# What Is Oud? A Beginner's Guide for Indian Buyers

Oud has a mystique unlike any other perfume ingredient. It is described as the "wood of the gods," priced higher than gold by weight, and wrapped in centuries of tradition from Assam to Arabia. It is also one of the most misunderstood — and most faked — materials in all of perfumery.

If you are curious about oud but intimidated by the prices and the jargon, this guide is for you.

## What Oud Actually Is

Oud (also spelled oudh) comes from the agarwood tree, primarily species in the Aquilaria genus. Here is the key fact most marketing skips: healthy agarwood is odourless. Oud only forms when the tree is infected by a specific mould, and the tree produces a dark, fragrant resin to fight the infection. That resin-saturated wood is agarwood. Oud oil is distilled from it.

This is why real oud is rare and expensive. Only a fraction of trees develop the infection, the trees take years to mature, and overharvesting has made wild agarwood scarce enough to be internationally regulated.

India has a deep connection to this story. Assam's agarwood — known as Hindi oud — is among the most prized varieties in the world.

## The Main Types of Oud

Oud is not one smell. The region, the species, and the distillation method all change the character dramatically.

**Hindi oud (Assam, India).** The powerhouse. Deep, animalic, leathery, sometimes barnyardy in the opening, drying down to a rich woody warmth. This is the oud of traditional attars and the most challenging for beginners — but also the most rewarding once your nose adjusts. If someone in India offers you "pure oud oil," this is most likely what they mean.

**Cambodian (Cambodi) oud.** Smoother and sweeter than Hindi, with honeyed and fruity facets. Often recommended as a starting point because it is powerful without being confrontational. Genuinely good Cambodian oil is expensive and increasingly rare.

**Laotian and Thai ouds.** Generally sit between Hindi and Cambodian in character — woody and warm with varying degrees of sweetness. Thai plantation ouds tend to be cleaner and more approachable.

**Papua New Guinean oud.** Earthy and green-leaning, sometimes with a mushroom-like quality. Distinctive and generally more affordable than the Asian varieties.

**Cultivated vs wild.** Wild oud is rarer, more complex, and vastly more expensive. Cultivated oud is more consistent and affordable. For a beginner, cultivated oil from a reputable seller is the sensible entry point.

## What Oud Smells Like (Honestly)

Forget the poetry for a moment. On first encounter, real oud oil often smells:

- **Medicinal and woody** — like an ancient wooden chest or a temple storeroom
- **Animalic** — leather, and in strong Hindi varieties, something close to a barnyard (this fades as it dries down)
- **Warm and resinous** — the drydown is where oud becomes beautiful: deep, smooth, enveloping woodiness that can last on skin for a full day

Many beginners are surprised or even put off by the opening. That is normal — the opening blast is not the point. The hours-long evolution is.

If your first "oud" smells sweet and simple from the first second, be suspicious. Real oud is rarely instantly likeable.

## Oud in Western Perfumes vs Real Oud Oil

This distinction confuses almost everyone at first.

Most designer "oud" perfumes — the ones on department store shelves — contain little or no real oud oil. They use synthetic oud accords: laboratory-created molecules that suggest oud's woody warmth at a fraction of the cost. Some are excellent. They are also not oud in the traditional sense.

Real oud oil is sold as attar (usually in small 3ml crystal bottles in India and the Gulf) or used in small percentages in ultra-expensive niche perfumes. When a ₹2,000 designer fragrance lists "oud" in its notes, you are smelling an interpretation, not the raw material.

Neither is illegitimate — but you should know which one you are buying.

## Fair Prices: What Should Oud Cost?

This is where beginners get hurt, so let us be direct.

**Pure oud oil** is genuinely expensive. Even cultivated Hindi oud oil of decent quality typically starts in the thousands of rupees for just 3ml, and good wild oil can cost many times more. If someone offers you "pure Assam oud oil" for a few hundred rupees, it is not pure oud oil. That is not snobbery — it is arithmetic. The raw material costs more than that.

**Oud attars and blends** are more accessible. Many Indian attar houses sell oud-based blends — real oud oil diluted in sandalwood oil or mixed with other notes — at prices ordinary buyers can afford. These are honest products when labelled honestly.

**Oud perfumes (Western style)** range from affordable to luxury. Here you are paying for the composition, not the raw oud content.

The golden rule: price is the most reliable authenticity signal. Real oud cannot be cheap. Anyone selling it cheap is selling something else.

## How to Buy Oud in India Without Getting Scammed

**Buy from established attar houses.** India — particularly Kannauj, the historic attar capital — has generations-old attar makers with reputations to protect. A house that has been selling to the same families for decades is far safer than a random Instagram seller.

**Start with small quantities.** Reputable sellers offer 1ml or 3ml bottles. Anyone pushing you toward large quantities of "pure oud" at suspicious prices is a red flag.

**Learn the smell before spending big.** Try oud-based attars and blends first. Visit a physical attar shop if you can — Kannauj, Old Delhi's Dariba Kalan, and Hyderabad's attar markets are real places where you can smell before buying.

**Be sceptical of superlatives.** "100% pure wild Hindi oud, best quality, cheapest price" is a contradiction. Sellers who are honest about their oil being cultivated, or a blend, are more trustworthy than sellers who claim everything is the finest wild grade.

**Understand the grades game.** Sellers love grading systems — "Grade A," "Super," "Royal." These grades are not standardised. One seller's "Super" is another's ordinary. Judge by smell, not by label.

**Avoid these red flags:** prices far below market, refusal to provide small samples, no physical address or verifiable business history, and pressure tactics ("last piece," "special price only today").

## How to Wear Oud

A practical note since you have come this far: oud oil is potent. One small swipe — traditionally applied with the small glass stick (dipstick) that comes in the bottle — behind each ear or on the wrists is enough. It projects for hours and lasts on clothes for days.

Oud also layers beautifully. A touch of Hindi oud under a fresh citrus fragrance creates a personal signature that is entirely your own. Experiment once you are comfortable with the material solo.

## The Bottom Line

Oud rewards patience. Do not rush to buy the most expensive oil you can find. Start with honest blends from reputable houses, train your nose, learn what regions you prefer, and upgrade when you know what you are paying for.

India is one of the best places in the world to begin this journey — the heritage is here, the markets are here, and the knowledge is here. You just need to approach it with open nostrils and healthy scepticism.
